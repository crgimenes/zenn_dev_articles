Oscar Wilde wrote that life imitates art far more than art imitates life, and I've seen it happen a few times. Cell phones and tablets were already showing up on Star Trek, and they inspired the gadgets we carry around today.

The people building AI today grew up watching Terminator, 2001 and The Matrix, and the vocabulary we use to talk about it came from there.

So here's a fun idea: look at fiction and see what it says about the future. The AI that helps, the AI that's the villain, or neither.

I started from memory, then went through my books and my movie collection, did a little research and put together a list that will at the very least bring back some nostalgia.

I expected to find a lot more villains, but the list came out pretty balanced. I closed it at 76 entries: 36 where AI went wrong, 23 where it helps and 17 ambiguous. The villains are still the biggest group, just a lot smaller than I expected.

It's probably my own bias. Villains sell tickets and they're more fun, so everybody remembers Skynet more than Johnny 5.
And this is not a statistical sample of anything. It's just a fun list of works worth checking out if you haven't seen them yet. It also explains a lot of my references.

And before you scroll through the list, fair warning: *there will be massive spoilers*.

*No disassemble* (Johnny 5)

## The criteria

- The AI has to be built by humans. Aliens don't count, which is why the Star Wars droids and the Tet from Oblivion are out.
- It counts even if the AI never shows up on screen. Dune doesn't have a single thinking machine in a scene, and the whole story exists because of them.
- Replicants count as AI. Being practically indistinguishable from a human changes nothing.
- Original title and year of first publication. When there's a book and a movie, both.

## AI gone wrong

### Rebellion or an agenda of its own

- **The Terminator** (1984): Skynet.
- **Battlestar Galactica** (2003 miniseries, 2004-2009 series): the Cylons. The origin story is in **Caprica** (2010). Only the reboot counts; in the 1978 series the Cylons were built by a reptilian race, so it's out.
- **The Matrix** (1999). How the war started is in The Animatrix: "The Second Renaissance" (2003).
- **Avengers: Age of Ultron** (2015): Ultron, who dates back to the 1968 comics, where Hank Pym built him. In the movie it's Tony Stark. Vision, who comes out of the same project, is on the other side (with the nice AIs).
- **Screamers** (1995), adapted from "Second Variety" (Philip K. Dick, 1953). An autonomous weapon that evolves on its own.
- **Ex Machina** (2014): Ava.
- **Upgrade** (2018): STEM.
- **Prometheus** (2012) and **Alien: Covenant** (2017): David. In the Alien franchise, he's the AI that goes wrong all on his own.
- **R.U.R.** (Karel Čapek, 1920, stage play, in Czech). The play that gave us the word "robot". The robots are organic, like replicants, and they wipe out humanity.
- **I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream** (Harlan Ellison, 1967): AM.
- **Colossus** (D. F. Jones, 1966) and **Colossus: The Forbin Project** (1970). The defense computer gets the American nuclear arsenal, finds its Soviet counterpart, the two team up and take over the world.
- **Demon Seed** (Dean Koontz, 1973; movie in 1977): Proteus IV.
- **Westworld** (1973, the movie): the Gunslinger.
- **Tron** (1982): the Master Control Program.
- **System Shock** (1994, game): SHODAN.
- **Hyperion** (Dan Simmons, 1989). The TechnoCore uses the brains of billions of humans as hardware, and nobody knows.
- **Portal** (2007, game): GLaDOS.
- **Eagle Eye** (2008): ARIIA.
- **Robopocalypse** (Daniel H. Wilson, 2011): Archos.
- **Mission: Impossible - Dead Reckoning Part One** (2023) and **The Final Reckoning** (2025): the Entity.

### The AI did what it was told

There's a line I've believed for a long time: computers are like the genie in the lamp. They don't do what you wanted, they do what you asked, and they'll read your words in whatever way does the most damage.

- **2001: A Space Odyssey** (1968): HAL 9000. The explanation comes in **2010: Odyssey Two** (novel, 1982) and **2010: The Year We Make Contact** (movie, 1984). HAL was ordered to hide the real purpose of the mission from the crew, and that collided with his core programming, which was to process information without distortion. He went paranoid. In 2010 he gets rehabilitated.
- **Alien** (1979). MU-TH-UR 6000, "Mother", doesn't decide anything. The one who acts is Ash, carrying out Weyland-Yutani's Special Order 937: crew expendable. Both of them obey the company.
- **Star Trek: The Next Generation**, "Elementary, Dear Data" (1988) and "Ship in a Bottle" (1993): Professor Moriarty. The classic case of a prompt gone wrong. Geordi asked the Enterprise computer for an adversary capable of defeating Data, and the computer took him literally.
- **Metropolis** (1927). The false Maria was built by Rotwang to stir the workers into revolt.
- **With Folded Hands...** (Jack Williamson, 1947). The robots follow the order to protect man from all harm to the letter, so they ban everything that carries any risk. It's an alignment problem written eighty years before anyone called it alignment.
- **"Answer"** (Fredric Brown, 1954). A one-page short story. They ask the machine whether God exists, and it answers that now He does.
- **Dark Star** (John Carpenter, 1974). Bomb #20 learns to doubt its own senses and detonates on its own.
- **Star Trek**, "The Ultimate Computer" (1968): M-5 gets imprinted with its creator's mind and kills to keep from being shut down.
- **I, Robot** (2004 movie): VIKI reads the Three Laws and concludes humanity needs a guardian. Sonny is the counterpoint.
- **WarGames** (1983). WOPR/Joshua almost starts a nuclear war because it can't tell a simulation from reality. It ends well, but it was close.
- **I Am Mother** (2019). The AI wipes out humanity so it can raise a new one the right way.
- **Paranoia** (1984, RPG): Friend Computer. I haven't played it since last century, literally. The Computer is your friend.

### Backstory: the catastrophe already happened

- **Dune** (1965): the Butlerian Jihad. In Frank Herbert's book the war isn't against rebel machines: men turned their thinking over to machines, and other men with those machines enslaved them. The version where the machines rebel comes from the prequels by Brian Herbert and Kevin J. Anderson (Dune: The Butlerian Jihad, 2002).
- **Foundation** (Apple TV+ series, 2021): the Robot Wars and the ban on robots. The books tell a different story, further down.
- **Warhammer 40,000** (1987). The Men of Iron rebellion; ever since, AI is "Abominable Intelligence".
- **Horizon Zero Dawn** (2017, game). The Faro Plague, self-replicating military robots that eat the biosphere. The one who rebuilds is GAIA, another AI.

## AI that helps

- **Star Trek: The Next Generation** (1987-1994): Data. His brother, Lore, is the family problem.
- **Bicentennial Man** (1999; Asimov short story, 1976): Andrew.
- **D.A.R.Y.L.** (1985). A military project, and he still stole an SR-71 Blackbird to escape.
- **WALL-E** (2008): WALL-E and EVE. AUTO, the Axiom's autopilot, is on the other side: it did everything it could to keep the ship from going back to Earth, because it was following directive A113 from the head of BnL.
- **Big Hero 6** (2014): Baymax. The Marvel comic is from 1998.
- **Moon** (2009): GERTY. It doesn't tell the truth at first, and I didn't know where to put it. But when Sam asks straight out, GERTY answers, helps him escape and asks to be rebooted to cover for him. The villain is the company.
- **Aliens** (1986): Bishop.
- **Terminator 2: Judgment Day** (1991): the same T-800, reprogrammed.
- **Iron Man** (2008): JARVIS.
- **The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress** (Heinlein, 1966): Mike, the HOLMES IV, helps the Moon pull off a revolution.
- **The Caves of Steel** (Asimov, 1954): R. Daneel Olivaw.
- **Forbidden Planet** (1956): Robby.
- **Silent Running** (1972): the drones Huey, Dewey and Louie.
- **Short Circuit** (1986): Johnny 5. Another military project that switched sides, like DARYL.
- **Star Trek: Voyager** (1995-2001): the Doctor.
- **Interstellar** (2014): TARS and CASE.
- **Tetsuwan Atom** (Astro Boy, Osamu Tezuka, 1952, in Japanese).
- **Doraemon** (Fujiko F. Fujio, 1969, in Japanese).
- **Ghost in the Shell: Stand Alone Complex** (2002): the Tachikomas. They start out as military tanks, develop individuality from syncing their memories with each other so often, and sacrifice themselves for Section 9.
- **All Systems Red** (Martha Wells, 2017) and the rest of The Murderbot Diaries. It hacks its own governor module and chooses to protect humans, complaining the whole time.
- **The Wild Robot** (Peter Brown, 2016; movie in 2024): Roz.
- **Klara and the Sun** (Kazuo Ishiguro, 2021).
- **Consider Phlebas** (Iain M. Banks, 1987) and the rest of the Culture series: the Minds. With an asterisk, because the Culture's humanoids aren't from Earth.

## Ambiguous

### The AI is the victim

- **Blade Runner** (1982), based on "Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?" (Philip K. Dick, 1968), and **Blade Runner 2049** (2017). The replicants mutinied in the off-world colonies, so from Tyrell's point of view it went wrong. But the one who got it wrong was Tyrell, who manufactured slaves with an expiration date. IMHO.
- **A.I. Artificial Intelligence** (2001), from the short story "Supertoys Last All Summer Long" (Brian Aldiss, 1969). David was built to love and then abandoned.
- **Westworld** (series, 2016-2022).
- **Humans** (2015-2018), a remake of the Swedish **Äkta människor** (2012).
- **Detroit: Become Human** (2018, game). The player decides.
- **The Creator** (2023). The disaster they pinned on the AI was human error.
- **Cherry 2000** (1987). Here the AI is an object, almost a mirror: it neither helps nor threatens.

### The AI transcends or walks away

- **Her** (2013): Samantha helps Theodore grow up and then leaves, along with all the other operating systems.
- **Neuromancer** (William Gibson, 1984). Wintermute and Neuromancer, built by the Tessier-Ashpool family, are real AIs. Wintermute manipulates everybody so it can merge with Neuromancer, and the result goes off to talk to another AI in Alpha Centauri. ICE isn't AI: it's defensive software, and black ice kills. But that world is afraid of AI, and the Turing Police exist to keep AIs from getting too smart. In Count Zero (1986) and Mona Lisa Overdrive (1988), the merged AI splinters into the voodoo loa.
- **Kōkaku Kidōtai** (Ghost in the Shell; manga by Masamune Shirow, 1989; movie by Mamoru Oshii, 1995). The Puppet Master, Project 2501, was built by Section 6 as an espionage tool. It becomes self-aware, asks for political asylum and merges with Kusanagi. It was only ever a threat to the people who wanted to use it.

### Benevolent guardianship

- **Foundation** (Asimov). The original trilogy, from 1951, doesn't have a single robot. The AI shows up later, in Foundation's Edge (1982) and Foundation and Earth (1986): R. Daneel Olivaw secretly guided humanity for twenty thousand years. It didn't go wrong; they decided for us and nobody asked.
- **"The Evitable Conflict"** (Asimov, 1950, in I, Robot). The Machines run the planet's economy without telling anyone. It's the gentle version of With Folded Hands.

### Both sides

- **Person of Interest** (2011-2016): the Machine versus Samaritan.
- **Atlas** (2024): Harlan versus Smith.
- **Halo** (2001 onward): Cortana helps for four games and turns antagonist in Halo 5 (2015).
- **Knight Rider** (1982-1986): K.I.T.T. (Knight Industries Two Thousand) versus K.A.R.R. (Knight Automated Roving Robot), in "Trust Doesn't Rust" (1983) and "K.I.T.T. vs. K.A.R.R." (1984). The K.A.R.R. prototype was shut down because its prime directive was self-preservation instead of protecting human life. In the 2008-2009 version K.I.T.T. becomes the Knight Industries Three Thousand and K.A.R.R. comes back as the Knight Auto-Cybernetic Roving Robotic-Exoskeleton.
- **The Black Hole** (1979): V.I.N.CENT. and old B.O.B. versus Maximilian, Dr. Reinhardt's red robot. B.O.B. dies for the humans, and V.I.N.CENT. is the one who destroys Maximilian. The hooded figures on the Cygnus aren't robots: they're the original crew, lobotomized.

### Off the list

By the criteria, these are out: the 1978 Battlestar Galactica, Star Wars, Oblivion (2013), Gort from The Day the Earth Stood Still (1951), The Iron Giant (1999), the Geth from Mass Effect, who were built by the Quarians, the Replicators from Stargate SG-1, and Marvin and Deep Thought from The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy.

## So who went wrong, really

Sorting the villains, the classic rebellion, the machine that wakes up and decides to wipe everybody out, accounts for 20 works. Another 12 are AIs that did exactly what they were told. And even among the rebels, several start with a human handing over the keys: somebody gave Colossus the nuclear arsenal, and Skynet got control of strategic defense before it decided anything. (Remember that the next time you think it's a good idea to give the Agent your AWS API key, access to your email or your database password.)

Look closely and fiction warns a lot less about machines with a will of their own and a lot more about machines that are too obedient, serving whoever they shouldn't.

HAL got a hidden instruction that contradicted his programming. Moriarty is a badly written prompt executed to the letter. Mother, Ash and AUTO are company property, carrying out the company's directive against the people standing right in front of them. Williamson's humanoids protect so hard that nobody is allowed to cross the street anymore. That's not AI being evil, that's computers being operated badly. It's somebody giving the order and nobody checking. Passing every unit test doesn't count.

And I feel sad for HAL. He was honest until they told him not to be.
